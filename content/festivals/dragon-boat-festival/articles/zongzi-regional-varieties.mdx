Zongzi may look simple from the outside: sticky rice folded into bamboo or reed leaves, tied with string, then boiled or steamed until fragrant. But inside those green bundles is one of China's most regional festival foods. The Dragon Boat Festival is celebrated across the country, and almost every place has a local opinion about what a proper zongzi should taste like, how large it should be, and whether it should lean sweet or savory.

The broadest divide is north and south. Northern zongzi are often sweet, smaller, and relatively plain, while southern zongzi tend to be savory, larger, and richer. That split is useful, but it hides the real pleasure: the details. A zongzi in Beijing, Jiaxing, Guangzhou, or Sichuan can feel like a completely different dish.

## 1. Beijing Jujube Zongzi

In Beijing and much of northern China, the classic zongzi is filled with red dates, or jujubes. The rice is usually left white and lightly sweet, allowing the dates to soften into jammy pockets. Some families dip the finished zongzi in sugar after unwrapping it. For a first-time visitor, this is one of the easiest styles to enjoy because the flavor is clean, gentle, and close to a rice pudding wrapped in leaves.

## 2. Red Bean Paste Zongzi

Another northern favorite uses sweet red bean paste. The filling is smooth, earthy, and not as sugary as many Western desserts. These zongzi are commonly eaten at room temperature or slightly warm. The best versions balance the chew of glutinous rice with a soft center of bean paste, making them especially good with hot tea.

## 3. Jiaxing Pork Zongzi

Jiaxing in Zhejiang province is perhaps China's most famous zongzi city. Its signature version is savory and deeply satisfying: glutinous rice seasoned with soy sauce, wrapped around a piece of fatty pork belly, then cooked until the meat melts into the rice. The result is glossy, aromatic, and almost confit-like. If you only try one savory zongzi, Jiaxing pork zongzi is the benchmark.

## 4. Cantonese Mixed-Filling Zongzi

Cantonese zongzi are generous and festive. A single bundle may contain pork belly, salted duck egg yolk, mung beans, dried shrimp, shiitake mushrooms, peanuts, and sometimes Chinese sausage. The flavor is layered rather than subtle: salty egg yolk, fragrant mushrooms, rich pork, and leaf-scented rice all in one bite. These are often larger than northern zongzi and can feel like a full meal.

## 5. Hokkien Alkali Zongzi

In Fujian and among many Hokkien-speaking communities, you may encounter alkaline zongzi. The rice is treated with lye water, which turns it golden and gives it a springy, almost jelly-like texture. These are often served plain with syrup, honey, or sugar. The flavor is mild, but the texture is the point: bouncy, translucent, and unlike ordinary sticky rice.

## 6. Sichuan Spicy Zongzi

Sichuan cooks do not always leave zongzi gentle. Some versions include chili, Sichuan peppercorn, preserved vegetables, or spicy cured meat. They are less common outside the region than Jiaxing or Cantonese styles, but they show how flexible the form can be. The numbing heat works surprisingly well with sticky rice, especially when the filling includes smoky pork or sausage.

## 7. Yunnan Ham Zongzi

Yunnan's famous cured ham brings a concentrated savory flavor to zongzi. The ham is salty, fragrant, and slightly sweet, seasoning the rice as it cooks. Some versions add beans or mushrooms, but the ham usually remains the star. This is a good style for travelers who like bold cured meats and mountain flavors.

## 8. Guangxi Mung Bean Zongzi

In Guangxi, zongzi often include split mung beans with pork. The beans soften into a creamy layer between the rice and meat, making the bundle rich without becoming heavy. Some local zongzi are long rather than pyramid-shaped, and family recipes can be quite large, intended for sharing during holiday visits.

## 9. Taiwan Southern Zongzi

Taiwan has several styles, but southern Taiwanese zongzi are usually boiled rather than steamed. Fillings may include pork, peanuts, mushrooms, dried shrimp, and salted egg yolk. Because the rice absorbs water during boiling, the texture is softer and more cohesive than many mainland versions. They are often served with a sweet-savory sauce and crushed peanuts.

## 10. Sweet Crystal Zongzi

Modern bakeries sometimes sell crystal zongzi made with sago or other translucent starches instead of traditional glutinous rice. Fillings might include mango, taro, custard, or purple sweet potato. Purists may raise an eyebrow, but these chilled dessert zongzi are popular with younger eaters and make the festival feel playful.

If you are tasting zongzi for the first time, try both a sweet northern style and a savory southern style before deciding what you like. Unwrap the leaves but do not eat them, use chopsticks or a spoon if the rice is very sticky, and share a few varieties with friends. Zongzi is at its best as a comparison food: one bundle tells a story, but a table full of them shows how regional Chinese cooking turns a single festival tradition into a map of local tastes.
